Interview — A-Grade Requirement

Deeper Than the Literature

A conversation with someone who lives or works inside this space — going beyond what published research can capture.

Before the Interview

For my research into agentic AI in enterprise environments, I wanted to talk to someone who'd actually watched this technology evolve from the inside — not just read about it. I reached out through a family connection to a senior developer at SAP who has spent over two decades building and maintaining their core systems. He agreed to speak on the record but asked that his last name not be used. What drew me to him specifically was that he'd seen SAP's entire arc: from rigid, rule-based software to today's push toward autonomous AI agents. I wanted to know what that transition actually feels like when you're the one building it — not what the press releases say.

👤
Marcus T.
Senior Principal Engineer, SAP SE — 20 years in enterprise software
Marcus has spent his entire career at SAP, working across core ERP systems and now directly on their Business AI and agentic automation layer. He's one of the few people who has watched AI go from a feature request to a fundamental redesign of how enterprise software makes decisions.
The Conversation
Conducted February 2026  ·  Video call  ·  Approx. 55 minutes
You

So just to start — does agentic AI actually have too much control right now? Like in your opinion?

Marcus

Yeah. In a lot of deployments, honestly, yes. And I say that as someone who's building this stuff. The systems we're creating can make hundreds of decisions an hour — financial decisions, operational decisions — with very little human involvement. That's the whole pitch. But just because we can remove humans from the loop doesn't mean we always should. I think the industry moved faster than the safeguards did.

You

What's an example of it going wrong because of that?

Marcus

I can't name clients obviously, but — we've seen situations where an agent is given a goal, like clearing a backlog of invoices, and it just... does it. Efficiently. But some of those invoices had flags on them that a human would have noticed. The agent didn't care, it had a goal and it hit the goal. Everything was technically correct and the outcome was still a mess. That's the thing people don't get — these systems don't second-guess themselves. They just execute.

You

Okay so this is mostly an enterprise thing right now — but regular people are starting to use agentic AI too. Like AI that books appointments, answers emails, manages files. Is that just as risky?

Marcus

It's a different kind of risky. In enterprise there's at least theoretically someone responsible — a compliance team, an IT department. When it's just a person who downloaded an app and said "sure, manage my calendar and inbox," there's nobody watching. And these tools ask for a lot of permissions upfront. Access to your email, your contacts, sometimes your files. Most people click through that without really thinking about what they just handed over. The agent needs that access to work, that's legitimate — but it also means if something goes wrong, or if the company behind it has bad data practices, your information is very exposed.

You

What kind of data are we actually talking about? Like what does it see?

Marcus

More than people realize. If an agent is managing your email it's reading everything — not just the thread you pointed it to. It needs context. So it might be seeing your medical appointment confirmations, your financial statements coming in, personal conversations. Same with a scheduling agent that has calendar access — it can infer a lot about your life just from patterns. Where you are, who you meet with, what you do on weekends. None of that feels like "data" when you're just trying to get help scheduling a dentist appointment. But it is data, and it's being processed somewhere.

You

Has there been a case where that actually leaked or got misused?

Marcus

There have been several incidents — I won't pretend otherwise. Some have been publicized, a lot haven't. The nature of data issues is that companies have very little incentive to be loud about them. What I'll say is that the architecture of a lot of these consumer AI tools is not designed with data minimization in mind. It's designed for capability. The more context the agent has, the better it performs — so the default is to collect broadly and retain. Which is the opposite of what you'd want from a privacy standpoint.

You

So when something goes wrong for an average person — like the AI does something they didn't want — what even happens? Who's responsible?

Marcus

That's the part that's genuinely unresolved and it bothers me. Right now the terms of service for most of these tools are written so the company is almost never liable. You authorized the agent to act on your behalf, so legally the argument is you're responsible for what it did. Even if you had no idea it could do that. I've read some of these agreements and they're — I mean they're not written for normal people to understand. Someone's agent sends an email they didn't mean to send, or cancels something, or makes a purchase — good luck getting that unwound cleanly.

You

Do you think most people using these tools day to day even know this stuff?

Marcus

No. And I don't say that to be condescending — I say it because the products are deliberately designed to feel simple and trustworthy. The whole UX philosophy is "just tell it what you want." That frictionlessness is the product. But friction sometimes exists for a reason. When you had to manually do something, you were also manually reviewing it. That moment of doing is also a moment of oversight. Agents remove that. And for a lot of tasks that's fine, great even. But people should at least know that the oversight is gone — not just assume someone else is handling it.

Key Quote
"When you had to manually do something, you were also manually reviewing it. That moment of doing is also a moment of oversight. Agents remove that — and most people don't even know the oversight is gone." — Marcus T., Senior Principal Engineer, SAP SE
