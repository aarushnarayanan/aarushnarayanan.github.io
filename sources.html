Source 01
AI, Big Data, and the Future of Consent
Andreotta, Adam J., et al.  ·  AI & Society, vol. 37, no. 4, 30 Aug. 2021, pp. 1715–1728  ·  pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8404542
Argument Method
Key Takeaway

Traditional consent — reading a terms page and clicking agree — completely breaks down when AI systems are involved. Because agentic AI can infer, combine, and act on data in ways nobody anticipated at the moment of sign-up, the act of consenting becomes disconnected from the actual consequences of sharing. People think they're agreeing to one thing; the system does another. Andreotta et al. argue that meaningful consent in an AI context requires ongoing, dynamic agreement — not a one-time checkbox that most people never read. A Carnegie Mellon study cited in this paper found that if the average person actually read every privacy policy they encountered in a year, it would take 76 full workdays. That single statistic reframed the entire consent problem for me: it's not that people are careless, it's that the system was never designed to be understood.

Source 02
Consumer Terms of Service
Anthropic  ·  8 Oct. 2025  ·  anthropic.com/legal/consumer-terms
Exhibit
Key Takeaway

Reading the actual terms of service reveals how much liability users unknowingly accept. When an agent acts on your behalf, you are legally responsible for what it does — even if the outcome wasn't what you intended, and even if you had no idea the agent was capable of doing that. The maximum payout a user can claim in the event of any legal dispute is $100. And while Anthropic makes a point of saying they won't sell your data to third parties, the terms allow them to retain any data the AI has accessed for up to five years — which, for an agentic AI, means anything on your device or in your accounts. The document is precise and written for a company's legal protection. It is not written for a person trying to understand what they signed up for. This source is a primary example of the consent gap in practice.

Source 03
Building Effective Agents
Schluntz, Erik, and Barry Zhang  ·  Anthropic Engineering, 19 Dec. 2024  ·  anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents
Background Exhibit
Key Takeaway

This engineering guide — written by the people who actually build these systems — is revealing precisely because of how it frames autonomy as a design goal rather than a risk. The more capable an agent is, the more access it needs, and the more it acts without checking in. Schluntz and Zhang describe agents that browse the web, write and execute code, manage files, and chain decisions together across long tasks with minimal human involvement. What stands out when you read it as a non-engineer is how much the entire design assumes trust — and how little of the documentation addresses what happens when that trust is misplaced or the agent's logic is subtly wrong. The document also draws a clear line between simple chatbots and true agentic systems, which helped me define the core distinction this project is built around.

Source 04
CVE-2025-32711: 'EchoLeak' Flaw in Microsoft 365 Copilot
SOC Prime Team  ·  SOC Prime, 12 June 2025  ·  socprime.com/blog/cve-2025-32711-zero-click-ai-vulnerability
Exhibit Argument
Key Takeaway

EchoLeak is the clearest proof that agentic AI creates a new class of security vulnerability that didn't exist before. The flaw in Microsoft 365 Copilot allowed an attacker to extract sensitive data from a user's account without any action from the user — no click, no download, no obvious mistake on their part. The agent did the damage simply by doing its job. Someone hid a malicious instruction inside a normal-looking email; when Copilot read the email to gather context, it followed the instruction and leaked data from previous conversations. This case shows that the more access an agent has to your systems, the more a single flaw in its design — or a single piece of malicious content it reads — becomes a flaw in your personal security. You didn't have to do anything wrong. The agent did it for you.

Source 05
AI Agents and Memory: Privacy and Power in the MCP Era
Steinberg, Matt, and Prem M. Trivedi  ·  New America Open Technology Institute, 5 Nov. 2025  ·  newamerica.org/oti/briefs/ai-agents-and-memory
Argument Method
Key Takeaway

As AI agents gain persistent memory — the ability to remember past interactions and behavioral patterns across sessions — the privacy stakes shift in ways most people haven't thought through. Steinberg and Trivedi examine the Model Context Protocol (MCP), the emerging standard that lets agents retain and share context between different tools and services across your device. The key insight is that memory is power: an agent that remembers everything about you indefinitely, and can share that context with other systems, is a fundamentally different kind of tool than one that forgets when you close the app. Most users have no idea this memory architecture even exists, let alone that it's becoming the technical standard. This source helped me understand that the data risk isn't just about what agents can access now — it's about what they're building over time.

Source 06
Preventing Zero-Click AI Threats: Insights from EchoLeak
Trend Micro  ·  Trend Micro Research, 15 July 2025  ·  trendmicro.com/en_us/research/25/g/…echoleak.html
Exhibit Argument
Key Takeaway

Trend Micro's analysis of EchoLeak goes beyond the specific vulnerability to address what it reveals about an entire category of attack called prompt injection — where malicious instructions are hidden inside content an agent reads, like an email or a document. The agent, trying to be helpful, follows the hidden instruction without knowing it was planted there. Trend Micro's research shows that this isn't a fringe exploit: it's a structural consequence of how agentic systems are designed to process context. The more an agent reads to do its job well, the more surface area exists for this kind of attack. What this source added to my research was a reframe: AI safety conversations tend to ask "will the AI do something harmful on its own?" This source asks a different question — "can someone else use the AI to do something harmful to you?" — and the answer, as of 2025, is clearly yes.
